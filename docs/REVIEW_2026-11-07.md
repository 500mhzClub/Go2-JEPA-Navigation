# Review: is piece-by-piece handover the road to a learned navigator? (7 November 2026)

The end goal: remove the hand-designed rules while raising performance and generalisation. The method since 26 October:
hand the hand-built planner's parts to the world model one at a time, each merged and measured before the next. This
review asks whether that method can reach the goal, using this repository's records (`docs/dynamic/PLAN.md`,
`docs/dynamic/STATUS.md`) and the literature on JEPA world models, embodied and social navigation, and self-driving.

*Revised the same day.* The first draft recommended a reinforcement-learning route: a privileged teacher distilled into
a policy, which would have left the world model as a feature extractor. That trades away the project's thesis, a
self-supervised system that learns representations and their dynamics from observation and uses them downstream. The
plan (section 6) now puts the world model in charge; the reinforcement-learning route is the fallback.

Run labels used below:
- **lv2**: the frozen lattice baseline, C1 [the ridge command-history forecaster] on the hand planner.
- **C5**: the planner with the world model (LeWM v2, a JEPA) forecasting the candidate commands.
- **Working setup**: C5 with the close-up rule, mover trails and the model's walker positions (`configs/working.yaml`).
- **Walkers that do not yield**: `obstacle_polite: off`; they walk up to the robot and wait for it to leave their path.

## Bottom line

1. **The goal is achievable, but not by this method.** The literature shows two routes:
   - **Learned policies trained in closed loop** have beaten hand rules. Examples: near-perfect PointGoal navigation from
     RL, self-play driving policies that beat the previous state of the art zero-shot on three benchmarks, learned
     yielding in social navigation.
   - **Self-supervised world models that plan** generalise to new layouts. This is the project's thesis, so far shown on
     single-agent, mostly static tasks.

   Neither route got there by swapping modules, one at a time, inside a rule-based planner.
2. **Every performance gain so far came from hand rules or the learned locomotion policy, never from the world model.**
   Held-out 81 → 112 → 125 of 140 came from the lattice and the close-up rule. Every handover to the model since
   1 November was neutral or negative:
   - walker positions: parity, and not load-bearing;
   - memory: negative in missions;
   - pose: no headroom;
   - the give-way response: blocked by sensing.
3. **We handed over the wrong things.** Each oracle study (25 October, P8, P10) found the limit is a *decision*: wait,
   give way, reroute or back out. The parts handed over were perception and state estimation, which have little headroom.
   The decision layer, which is almost all of the hand rules, never became the object of learning.
4. **The hand rules cannot be removed one at a time.** They are coupled: latches, close-up, trails, recovery and dispatch
   vetoes compensate for each other. A learned part is judged by how well it fits interfaces built for the hand parts.
   P9's memory broke the footprint checks; P11's retreat collided with the clearance and turn rules.
5. **Two limits no model can learn around.**
   - **Sensing.** With the cameras mounted 0.35 m forward and depth starting at 0.2 m, a walker parked 0.62 m away is
     invisible to depth, to the model's walker head and to visual odometry. The real Go2 carries a 360°×90° LiDAR with a
     0.05 m blind spot.
   - **The test bed.** One maze family, one scripted walker model, and a deadlock built into the non-yielding walker.
     Generalisation has never been tested outside that family.
6. **Recommendation: keep the goal and the thesis, change the method.** Make the world model the decision-maker:
   - learn representation and dynamics from observations that contain the interactions (the scripted corpus barely
     does);
   - add a small hard-wired intrinsic cost and a trained critic;
   - plan in latent space with H-JEPA's hierarchy.

   The first gate is offline, before any mission: on new, interaction-rich data, the model must predict that retreating
   clears a waiting walker (P4e's give-way probe, strengthened). If it cannot, the thesis has a clean negative result,
   and the reinforcement-learning route is the fallback.

   Scripted drivers, the hand planner and RL policies may collect data or serve as upper bounds, never as training
   targets. The piece-by-piece discipline (one change per merge, gates set beforehand, the stop rule) stays; the first
   piece is now the decision layer itself.

## 1. What the system is today

| Part | Hand or learned | What it is worth, from our records |

| Routing, frontiers, coverage, 32 selection stages, recovery family, close-up, trails, give-way, vacate, dispatch veto (`go2nav/planning`, 3.1k lines, about 118 tuned constants) | Hand | Every mission-level gain: lattice rounds, mover trails, close-up |

The world model's latent never reaches a decision except through two readouts: 0.8-s ego-motion forecasts, scored
against a hand-chosen waypoint and hand clearance checks, and walker positions. Neither is load-bearing.

The pattern since 1 November: four pieces, four results that did not move round trips. Each was honest and quick to
reach, thanks to the gates and the stop rule. But the list of remaining pieces is now the decision layer, and the
method has no way to hand that over one rule at a time.

## 3. Why piece-by-piece stalled

1. **Perception parts were handed over, but decisions are the bottleneck.** Every oracle study pointed at the decision.
   The order of pieces followed oracle gains, and the gains of the perception pieces were close to zero.
2. **Interfaces built for hand parts.** A learned part has to speak the hand pipeline's language: disc cells in a
   footprint check, positions in a stand-off rule, a pose for a hand map. P9's remembered walkers were more accurate
   offline, and they still blocked every turn exit and swung legs into walls. The self-driving survey (Chen et al., TPAMI
   2024) names the same thing: a modular cascade cannot optimise the parts jointly for the plan.
3. **Coupled rules.** Close-up, trails, latches, deadlock escape, terminal burst, dispatch vetoes and the clearance check
   compensate for one another. P11 showed it: a retreat triggers stand-off logic, clearance vetoes and the normal
   planner's own reversing arcs, which then hit walls. Removing or adding one rule moves the failure to the next.
4. **The model was never trained for the decision.** Corpus v2 comes from scripted drivers. With a mover waiting near and
   ahead, the robot holds or turns in 93% of the next 1.6 s, so the model learns "a waiting walker stays, a stopped robot
   stays". That is the inertia problem of behaviour cloning (Codevilla et al. 2019): causal confusion from data that
   lacks interventions. The JEPA literature adds that these objectives drift to slow features (Sobal et al. 2022). The
   P4e inverted rankings are exactly this.
5. **Horizon.** Forecasts cover 0.8 s; giving way takes 5-30 s. H-JEPA v2 (levels at 1.6 s and 12.8 s) has never run a
   mission. The JEPA planning papers report that flat planning collapses over long horizons. FF-JEPA's flat LeWM baseline
   reached 3.5% on PushT, and H-JEPA's hierarchy raises Visual AntMaze from 18% to 73%. A value function supplies the horizon in
   TD-MPC2 and in value-guided JEPA planning.
6. **Sensing.** The cameras look forward only, and depth starts at 0.2 m from lenses mounted 0.35 m ahead of the centre.
   Out-of-view contacts (rear calves), the stand-off blind zone and VO loss all follow from this. No model can learn to
   see what no sensor measures. The real Go2's L1 LiDAR (360°×90°, 0.05 m blind spot) would cover all three.
7. **The test bed.** It has one maze family (tree mazes, 1.3 m pitch) and kinematic cylinders with two scripted
   behaviours. The non-yielding walker is a deadlock by construction: it waits forever, 0.62 m away. The hand rules were
   tuned on mazes 00-29 and held out on 30-49 of the same generator, which shows in-family generalisation only. A learned
   policy trained on this test bed would overfit it just as the rules may.
8. **Measurement resolution.** An 80-mission round-trip bar passes 89% of the time if a change does nothing, and 35% if
   it costs 5 missions. Most handovers could only be judged "at parity", which gives no signal for improving the model.
9. **Throughput.** Genesis runs about 3.3× real time across 6 missions (74 missions in 2 h 57 min), about 3 million
   frames (0.7 million plans) a day. Model-free RL on the full stack would need 10^8-10^9 steps (DD-PPO took 2.5 billion
   frames to "solve" PointGoal), out of reach here. Recording observations is cheaper: corpus v2's 4,000 units took
   about 13 hours on the CPU. That suits learning from observation and planning with the result.

## 4. Is the goal achievable?

There are two routes.

**Route 1, the best evidence: learned policies trained in closed loop.** This is the fallback.
- DD-PPO reaches near-perfect PointGoal navigation from RL alone, with 90% of the final result at 100 million steps.
- GigaFlow's self-play policy, trained only in a simple batched simulator with no human data, beats the state of the art
  zero-shot on CARLA, nuPlan and Waymax.
- Habitat 3.0's RL social-navigation policy learns to back away to let a person through a door: our stand-off.
- Learned crossing policies in narrow passages beat ORCA and a rule-based yielding agent, which deadlock or lose about
  30 s (Kathuria et al. 2025).
- The freezing-robot problem, a planner that treats people as obstacles and stops, is solved by modelling the
  interaction, not by better obstacle avoidance (Trautman & Krause 2010).

**The caveats are as important.**
- Learned planners trained offline by imitation lose to a simple rule-based planner in closed loop. On nuPlan, PDM-Closed
  beat every learned method (Dauner et al. 2023). Imitation alone would not beat our hand planner.
- End-to-end pixel policies transfer badly. In real homes their success fell from 77% to 23%, while a learned policy on a
  map held up at 90% (Gervet et al. 2023). Keep structured intermediates (map, pose, tracks) as the policy's input, at
  least at first.
- Teacher-student distillation loses performance unless the student's representation is aligned with the teacher's
  (Learning by Cheating; Coaching a Teachable Student).
- Self-driving still keeps explicit safety layers, and the RL navigation literature uses safety filters: CBF filters, or
  filters used only during training.

**Route 2, the thesis: a self-supervised world model that plans.** This is the recommended route.

LeCun's architecture for it has six parts: perception, a world model learned from observation, a hard-wired intrinsic
cost, a trainable critic that predicts future cost, a planner that searches actions through the world model, and an
actor that later amortises the planner [A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence, 2022]. The evidence so far:
- DINO-WM plans zero-shot in latent space on mazes and pushing, and generalises to new maze layouts.
- PLDM, a JEPA latent dynamics model with planning, generalised best of six methods to unseen layouts from reward-free
  offline data, at about 100 times the evaluation cost of model-free policies.
- V-JEPA 2-AC picks and places zero-shot after about 62 hours of robot video.
- Navigation World Models plans by simulating or ranking trajectories, and can add constraints at planning time.
- Hierarchy fixes the horizon: H-JEPA raises Visual AntMaze from 18% to 73%.
- A value in the latent sharpens the cost (value-guided JEPA planning).
- Proprioception and multi-step rollout training matter (Terver et al. 2025).

The gap is ours to test. Every one of these is single-agent, and almost all are static or quasi-static. None plans
through agents that react to the robot.

Route 1's caveats apply here too:
- the model learns only the dynamics its data show (inertia);
- open-loop scores mislead, so missions decide;
- structured inputs such as the route and the map can stay until the model can replace them.

**What is not achievable** is removing the rules by improving the inputs to the rules.

## 5. Techniques to use, mapped to our failures

| Our failure | Technique | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Stand-offs; the robot freezes | Plan with a model of how walkers respond to the robot, not with walkers as obstacles | Freezing robot (Trautman & Krause). Interaction-aware RL policies learn it too (CrowdNav SARL; Habitat 3.0) |
| Model predicts inertia, rankings inverted | Observations with varied interventions; iterate plan, collect, retrain | DAgger's insight (learn on the states your own behaviour visits); ChauffeurNet's synthesised perturbations; Codevilla's inertia analysis |
| No learned decision; rules coupled | Planning in latent space against an intrinsic cost and a trainable critic; an amortised actor later | LeCun 2022 (APTAMI); TD-MPC2; value-guided JEPA planning (Destrade et al. 2025) |
| 0.8-s horizon | Hierarchical subgoals; a critic at the end of the rollout | H-JEPA; HWM (Bardes et al. 2026); TD-MPC2 |
| Rollouts unconditional on the robot's plan | Action-conditioned prediction of others: "what does the walker do if I back off?" (the give-way probe) | PiP; conditional behaviour prediction; Scene Transformer |
| Planning with JEPA latents | Proprioception in, multi-step rollout training, L2 cost; CEM or MPPI in latent | Terver et al. 2025; V-JEPA 2-AC; DINO-WM; PLDM |
| World model only forecasts ego motion for a hand pipeline | The planner, its cost and its critic read the world model's latent directly | MILE (world model and driving learned together); Think2Drive (planner trained inside a latent world model) |
| Blind zone, rear blindness | 360° range sensing as an observation, as on the real robot | Unitree Go2 (L1 LiDAR) |
| Metrics too coarse | Sequential tests; time, stand-off and contact-rate metrics; scenario suites | Francis et al. 2023 (social navigation evaluation); Li et al. 2024 (metrics can mislead) |
| Safety while learning | A contact reflex at dispatch, its interventions counted; contact in the intrinsic cost | Safety-filter literature (CBF filters) |
| If the thesis fails its gate | A privileged teacher trained by RL in a fast abstract simulator, distilled with DAgger | Learning by Cheating; CaT; GigaFlow; DD-PPO |

## 6. The plan: the world model as the decision-maker

**The principle.** The world model learns representation and dynamics from observation alone. The robot's behaviour
comes from planning with it against a small intrinsic cost: perception, world model, intrinsic cost, trainable critic
and planner, with an actor that amortises the planner later if speed demands it (LeCun 2022). Hand rules leave as the
planner takes over their decisions. Scripted drivers, the hand planner and RL policies may collect data or give upper
bounds; nothing is trained to imitate them. Each phase keeps the project's discipline: its own branch, gates set before
scoring, the stop rule, and a merge before the next.

**Phase A: observations that can show the problem** (1-2 days, mostly CPU, plus about 3 GPU-hours)
- **Range sensing.** A sensor modelled on the Go2's L1 (360°×90°, 0.05 m minimum range), recorded as an observation and
  fed to the encoder as a polar range image beside the camera frames. The hand reference gets it too, in its obstacle
  map and dispatch check, so that a gain from seeing more is not credited to the model.
  - Measure, no pass mark: P10's 80 missions with walkers that do not yield, run on the hand reference with the new
    sensor, against `wm_rude_model_*` [P8's run, 11 round trips]. It says how much of the failure is sensing, and it
    is the reference that the later phases are measured against.
- **Walkers.** A reactive pedestrian model with randomised parameters (ORCA or social force: patience, yielding share,
  passing where there is room), beside the two scripted ones.
- **Layouts.** A second family (rooms, open areas, corridors 1.0-2.0 m wide), and one family held out for good, never
  seen in data collection, tuning or training.
- **Metrics.** Sequential tests under the stop rule, plus time to goal, stand-off seconds and contacts per kilometre; one
  fixed anchor per suite.

**Phase B: data that contains the decision** (about half a day of collection on the CPU)
- **Coverage drivers,** extending `GiveWayDriver`. They approach walkers, then choose at random among:
  - holding for 2-30 s;
  - retreating 0.3-3 m along the robot's own path;
  - stepping into a side passage;
  - turning away and walking off;
  - advancing.

  Free exploration, and the hand planner's missions with added noise, keep ordinary navigation covered. All walker
  models and both layout families are used, except the held-out family. The drivers only make sure the outcomes get
  observed; nothing imitates them.
- **Recorded:** frames, range images, applied commands, proprioception and contacts. True walker states and the
  walkers' internal flags are recorded for probes only.
- **Gate on the data, before any training.** A stand-off window here means a walker still within 0.7 m, with the robot
  ahead of it.
  - At least 25% of stand-off windows are followed by the walker leaving the robot's path within 10 s. In corpus v2
    the robot holds in 93% of such moments, and almost none resolve.
  - At least 20% of stand-offs include a retreat of 1 m or more.

**Phase C: a model that predicts the interaction** (about 1-1.5 GPU-days of training, then probes on the CPU)
- **Model.** H-JEPA trained jointly end to end, with levels at 100 ms, 1.6 s and 12.8 s, on corpus v2 plus Phase B.
  - Inputs: frames, range image, proprioception (Terver et al. found proprioception helps), and the commands as
    actions.
  - Training: on multi-step rollouts (2-6 steps), not only teacher forcing.
  - Heads on the latent, for probes and the intrinsic cost: contact, ego motion, walker presence.
- **Gates.** Offline, on held-out units including the held-out layout family, set before scoring. This phase decides
  the thesis.
  1. **Give-way.** This is P4e's probe, strengthened. The retreat-minus-hold contrast must rank the walkers that respond
     with AUC at least 0.80, at 1.6 s and at 3.2 s.
     - 0.80 is the one-line rule's level. The privileged ceiling is 0.87; past models scored 0.19-0.40, inverted.
     - At the 12.8-s level: whether the robot's way is clear after a 2-3 m retreat, AUC at least 0.75.
  2. **Contact.** The contact head predicts held-out contacts (AUC at least 0.9 at 0.8 s), so that contact can enter the
     cost. On C5 the contact logit is fixed at -1000 today.
  3. **Own motion at standstill.** The one-step motion readout's bias stays under 1 mm per frame (P10 found 3-4 mm), so
     the model's own motion can be trusted over seconds.
  4. **Walkers.** Localisation no worse than LeWM v2's (0.216 m).
- **If gate 1 fails** on data that contain the interaction, the thesis has a clean negative result: the self-supervised
  predictor does not learn the reaction even when it is observed. The next choice is then supervision on predicted
  states (P4d's labels on rollouts) or route 1, and the record says which and why.

**Phase D: cost, critic and a latent planner on the stand-off suites** (about a week)
- **Intrinsic cost,** hard-wired and small: predicted contact, progress towards the route's next waypoint, and time.
  These are objectives, not behaviour rules, and they stay fixed while everything else is learned.
- **Critic.** It predicts discounted future intrinsic cost from the latent. It is trained by temporal differences on
  the corpus and on the model's own rollouts, and carries the horizon past the rollout.
- **Planner.** CEM or MPPI over command sequences within the locomotion policy's range:
  - 1.6-s rollouts at level 1;
  - level-2 and level-3 predictions as subgoals;
  - the critic at the end of each rollout.

  The dispatch contact veto stays as a reflex, and its interventions are counted.
- **What it replaces:** the selection stages, close-up, the stand-off use of tracks and trails, give-way, vacate, the
  recovery family and the latches. **What stays for now:** routing to the goal, the map, visual odometry.
- **Missions** on the validation mazes: walkers that do not yield (80), the fixed record (80, polite), four walkers (40).
  Bars set before scoring, against Phase A's reference with the same sensor:
  - walkers that do not yield: round trips at least the reference's plus 10, with wins at least twice the losses;
  - the fixed record: within 3;
  - contact stops: no more than the reference's.

  Missions are synchronous, so planning cost slows wall time only. A budget of 1 s per plan with six missions running
  keeps a suite near 6 hours.
- **Then held-out once,** including the held-out layout family.

**Phase E: hand the rest to the model** (later, one piece at a time)
- What the planner still takes from hand parts goes one input at a time, each removal its own merge:
  - the pose, from the model's own motion if gate 3 holds (P10's carrier work);
  - the walkers near the body (the deferred memory work, now as the latent's own state);
  - routing, from level-3 subgoals with a memory of the layout.

  This is where piece-by-piece fits: removing an input the planner has learned not to need.
- An amortised policy, distilled from the planner's own choices (LeCun's "mode 2" into "mode 1"), only if planning is
  too slow for the real robot. Its teacher is the world model's planner, not an outside agent.

**Fallback and tools.** If Phase C or D fails its gate, route 1 is the fallback: a privileged teacher trained by RL in a
fast abstract simulator, distilled with DAgger. Some of its parts can serve this route without becoming targets. An RL
policy or the hand planner can drive data collection, and privileged agents give upper bounds.

**Compute.**
- Phase A: 1-2 days, mostly CPU, plus about 3 GPU-hours for the reference.
- Phase B: about half a day of collection. Corpus v2's 4,000 units took about 13 hours, and the give-way top-up's 1,200
  about 5.
- Phase C: about 1-1.5 GPU-days for the joint H-JEPA, plus CPU probes.
- Phase D: critic training in hours; missions 6-20 hours a suite, depending on planning cost.
- Mind the R9700 faults recorded in memory (the LayerNorm bug, compiled-loss corruption) and the system drive.

## 7. Keep, stop, decide

**Keep:**
- the simulator, mission evaluation, oracle method, gates and stop rule, corpus tools;
- the learned locomotion policy, and the world model's lineage (LeWM, SIGReg, H-JEPA's joint training);
- the hand planner, frozen, as the reference (with the new sensor) and as one of the coverage drivers.

**Stop:**
- handing perception modules to the model inside the rule planner;
- adding hand rules. P11's vacate rule stays off; its findings (stand-offs happen in the cameras' blind zone; the robot
  is wedged at corners; true pose does not rescue it) are recorded as P11's negative result in STATUS;
- training the model to imitate anything. Drivers, the hand planner and RL policies collect data or bound results.

**Decisions for the user:**
1. Adopt the world-model plan (phases A-E), with Phase C's give-way gate as the test of the thesis and route 1 as the
   fallback.
2. The sensor: an L1-style range sensor as standard, for the model and the hand reference alike.
3. The test bed: the walker models, the layout families, and which family is held out for good.
4. If Phase C's gate fails: supervision on predicted states (P4d's labels on rollouts) or route 1.
